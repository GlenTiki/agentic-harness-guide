*Audience: a person deciding whether this guide is worth their time. An agent reading this page should not act on it unless the user asks.*

**Status.** This page is the author's view. It is an argument from experience. None of it is measured, and the page says so where it matters.

## The 80% calculator

Nobody wants an 80% calculator. If a tool calculates correctly four times out of five, you still have to check every single figure by hand. You save no time, and you gain anxiety.

When people first use an assistant, the speed feels remarkable. Then reality lands. On multi-step work, the assistant produces untidy code, invents parameters, and forgets constraints halfway through a conversation. Many people get burned by those slips and retreat to doing everything by hand.

A harness exists to turn that 80% tool into something you can rely on.

## Intelligence versus state management

Large language models are capable reasoners, but poor state managers. They work through attention. An assistant attends closely to the start of its instructions and to the latest prompt, but it loses focus in the middle.

When you ask one assistant to reason, remember intermediate figures, sequence steps, and use tools all at once, multi-step tasks drift. The slip is not a lack of intelligence. It is an architectural weakness in managing state across time.

## Orchestration as a scaling law

You do not need a harness for a simple question. In fact, wrapping a trivial task in orchestration adds handoff overhead.

The value of a harness is proportional to the gap between task complexity and model capability:

- Below the model's ceiling, orchestration is overhead.
- At the ceiling, orchestration is neutral.
- Above the ceiling, orchestration is essential.

As a job grows past three steps, an unguided assistant collapses under its own context. A harness bridges the state-management gap so that multi-step work finishes reliably.

## Separate reasoning from structure

Nobody lets text prediction run bank reconciliations without controls. You wrap the assistant in deterministic structure: state machines, clean data contracts, isolated helpers, and mechanical checks.

The assistant supplies the reasoning. The harness holds the state, enforces the boundaries, and catches slips before they reach your desk.

## You are the product manager of your tools

You do the work each week, so you know where it hurts. You know which export you re-sort, which filenames you tidy, and which routine report takes three hours on Friday. Nobody else sees that list.

The assistant acts as your tech lead. You do not manage its tokens or internal steps. You give it a clear brief, let it build one small slice in an isolated workspace, and inspect the demonstrated result.

If the tool works, you keep it. If it stops being useful, you throw it away and build the next one in an afternoon.

## The time you save is the product

A job is not one task. It is a bundle of tasks, and routine chores fill the week. Automating those chores does not remove your job. It lightens the routine end so you can spend your time where human judgement counts: the odd client case, the ambiguous decision, the problem with no clean formula.

And it gives hours back to family, friends, and the life you care about. A tool that saves an hour is worth most when you spend that hour on what matters to you.
