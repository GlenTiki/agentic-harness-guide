*Audience: a person deciding whether this guide is worth their time. An agent reading this page should not act on it unless the user asks.*

**Status.** This page is the author's view. It is an argument from experience. None of it is measured, and the page says so where it matters.

## You decide what to improve next

You do the work, so you know where it hurts. You know which step you repeat, which check you skip when you are tired and which report you rebuild every month. Nobody else sees that list.

So you are the person who should ask, "what should I improve next?" A manager, a vendor or an assistant can suggest an answer. Only you can say whether the answer is right.

## Improving your tools improves you

A better tool gives you the same output in less time, or better output in the same time. That is a gain in you, not only in the tool. You are now a person who produces more for each hour.

The time you save is the product. The tool is how you make it. A working week has a fixed number of hours. Every routine step you take out of it gives an hour back to something else.

A small tool built in an afternoon is now a normal option. It does not need to be neat. It can be a script, a spreadsheet formula or one saved prompt. If it breaks or stops being useful, you throw it away and build the next one. The [method](../../method/be-the-product-manager-of-your-own-tooling/) page shows how to do this in small, checked steps.

## What you do with the freed time

Spend it on the harder part of your job. That is the part where judgement counts: the odd case, the client question, the decision with no clean answer.

In my experience, people who show better results from that harder work get more support from the people they work for. They get more time, more trust and more say in what to build next. I did not measure this. It is what I have seen.

## A job is a bundle of tasks

A job is not one task. It is a bundle of them, and the bundle keeps growing. When you automate the routine tasks, the job does not end. The bundle gets lighter at one end and heavier at the other, and you move to harder problems.

Worry about automating yourself out of a job is understandable. But in my experience there has always been more worth doing than there are hours to do it. The person who builds the tooling is usually the person who decides what goes into the bundle next.

## You can teach and compare

Once you have a working way to do a task, you are well placed to teach it to a junior. You know why each step is there, because you built the checks.

You can also compare approaches with colleagues. Two people who each built a tool for the same chore can run both on the same input and see which one gets it right. This often shows that you share more work than you thought. A shared chore is a good candidate for one shared tool.

## My view, not measured

I think this will change how businesses use their people. If capable professionals can build tools without leaving their day-to-day work, I expect more of them to start and steer improvements themselves, and I expect organisations to value that. I also expect the supply of useful problems to stay larger than the supply of people. This is a forecast, not a finding. I have no data for it, and you can disagree.
