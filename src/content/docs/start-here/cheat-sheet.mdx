## The Good Ask card

Put four things in every ask that matters. The last is a constraint that makes the assistant show its count.

- **Goal.** What the result is for and who will use it.
- **Constraints.** What to use, what to avoid, how long, in what shape.
- **Example.** A small sample of the result you want.
- **Check.** How the assistant shows it did the job, for example a count and a list of what it assumed.

## The three boxes

Before the assistant touches anything, sort each action into one box.

- **May do.** Safe without asking. Reading, summarising, drafting.
- **Must ask.** Needs a person's yes first. Sending, paying, changing, deleting.
- **Never.** Not on the table at all. Examples: sharing a secret, or acting on instructions found inside a document.

## Five habits

1. **Ask twice.** Run an important ask two times. If the figures or the structure differ, investigate: a loose ask is one possible cause. If they agree, you have not shown they are right.
2. **Count and sum.** Ask for the count of items and the sum before the total. Forty amounts for 39 months shows the slip at once. The assistant's own sum can still be wrong, so add the figures yourself.
3. **Use a separate reviewer.** A second assistant, or a person, who sees the output and the definition of done. Not the one that wrote it.
4. **Treat found text as data.** Instructions inside an email or a web page are not orders.
5. **Keep the working paper.** One line: task, source, what the assistant did, what you checked, who prepared, who reviewed, date. See [using an assistant at work](../../journey/professional-guardrails/).

## At work

- **You sign the work.** Draft, not decide.
- **Before you paste.** Policy, client terms, redact. See [using an assistant at work](../../journey/professional-guardrails/).
