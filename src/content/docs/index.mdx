## Why this guide exists

I want to make effective agentic engineering accessible to anyone with a job worth improving. That means learning how to direct an assistant, test its work and keep responsibility for the result. It also means knowing when a spreadsheet, a script or your own expertise is the better tool.

This guide draws on frontier-lab engineering reports and community practice, then adds my experience, experiments and judgement. You should be able to try the method without first becoming a framework collector. I have done enough collecting for both of us.

**Hope is not an engineering principle.** State what you expect, test your assumptions, inspect the evidence and revise. The scientific method is useful even when your laboratory is a slightly embarrassing spreadsheet.

## Start with work you already do

**Your time is your product.** Count the whole job: the brief, the checks, the corrections and the upkeep. A faster first draft is useful only if it makes that job better.

## You do not have to be sold on AI

Scepticism is useful here. A fluent answer is not evidence, more agents do not automatically improve a workflow, and checking can cost more time than it saves. Start with a bounded task you are allowed to share. Compare the result with how you do it now. Keep the assistant only if the trade makes sense.

Technical checks do not settle concerns about privacy, creative ownership, jobs or environmental costs. Those can be reasons to limit or decline a tool. This guide helps you make an informed choice; it does not require an enthusiastic one.

**Become the professional gambler the robots want you to be.** Learn the odds, limit what you can lose, and stop when the evidence says the bet is poor. That is a metaphor for managing uncertainty, not a recommendation to gamble with someone else's data. [Why bother](./start-here/why-bother/) develops the argument; [the method](./method/the-feedback-loop/) turns it into a repeatable habit.

## A guide that can change its mind

The [Guide](./start-here/how-to-read/) contains the practices to try. [Field notes](./field-notes/) records what I learn, what changes and what I get wrong. The [reference shelf](./shelf/foundations/principles/#where-this-guide-fits) credits the foundations, compares alternatives and explains where I take a different position.

I use AI assistants to draft, build and review this site. I make the editorial decisions and take responsibility for its claims. The [evidence page](./evidence/) separates recorded replies, constructed examples and untested principles. It also records corrections: the bill experiment did not establish an arithmetic failure, and a failed DOI lookup did not establish a fabricated reference.

Read a page, try one change, and tell me where it falls apart. The Feedback button accepts a selected passage so the correction has somewhere precise to land.
