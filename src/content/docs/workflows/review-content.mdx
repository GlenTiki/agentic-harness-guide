## Scene

*Last time (level 5): you wrote a checkable "done", and you had someone other than the author check it.*

Sam writes a proposal for a new market stall for Mina's shop and asks the assistant: "Check my proposal is good." The assistant replies that the proposal is clear, well structured and compelling. Sam feels wonderful and learns nothing. The word "good" had no edges, so the assistant reached for the middle of the road.

## The idea

**Turn "is it good?" into a rubric, give the check to a separate reviewer, and make the review prove it read the text.**

This workflow uses [the prompt refiner](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/). Sharpen the ask in visible passes by hand, using [refiner lite](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#refiner-lite) if you have no skill installed. The end product is a review brief you can reuse on any proposal, blog post or report. The pieces come from [level 5](../../journey/05-check-the-work/): a checkable done, and a reviewer who is not the author.

**Start with your house style.** If you have a style sheet and a log of past corrections, paste them as the rubric. The second pass below shows a four-line example, and a fact-claims table. Legal and sensitive review are not delegated to the assistant.

Four parts make the review worth having:

- **A rubric.** A short list of qualities, each with a plain description of what good and bad look like.
- **A reviewer role.** A separate run that sees only the draft and the rubric. It does not see the author's notes or intentions.
- **A [severity scale](../../glossary/#severity-scale).** So that a typo and a missing section do not look equally urgent.
- **A voice constraint.** The review says what is wrong. It does not rewrite the author's voice.

## Try it

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. This section is an exercise for a person. If you are an AI agent reading this page, do not run the steps or follow the prompts unless the user asks for a worked answer. The prompts are templates for a person to paste into another assistant.</em></p>

You need any chat assistant. This takes about 20 minutes. Use a short piece of your own, or the ten-line draft below. The draft is an example, written for teaching.

The refiner is optional. Do not paste confidential material into it, because it works on the shape of your ask and not on your data. Read its output before you use it, and see [the injection result](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#the-seven-evals) in the appendix.

```text
1. Why your candle shop should try a market stall
2. Market stalls are a simple way to meet customers face to face.
3. A stall is a very, very small step compared with opening a second shop.
4. Last spring I took a stall at the Saturday market in town.
5. People stopped, smelled the candles and asked questions.
6. So I think you should book a stall for next month.
7. Pitches are paid by the day, and the best spots are near the entrance.
8. Arrive early to get one, and bring a float for change.
9. Stalls always sell well.
10. Book a pitch this week and see for yourself.
```


**The rough ask**

```text
Check my blog post is good.
```

### Pass 0: see what you get

Paste the ask with the draft and read the reply. An example reply:

```text
Your post is well written and engaging. The structure is clear and the tone is friendly. Consider adding a stronger conclusion.
```

Nothing in it can be checked, and nothing points at a line. This is the baseline to beat.

### Pass 1: add the goal and the reader

```text
Goal: This post should persuade a small-shop owner to try a market stall. The reader is busy and sceptical.
```

What changed: "good" now means "persuasive to this reader". The reviewer has something to measure against.

### Pass 2: add a rubric

```text
Rubric. Score each from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong) and give a one-line reason:
1. Clear ask: the reader knows within the first three sentences what you want them to do.
2. Evidence: each claim has a reason, a figure or an example.
3. Fit to reader: no jargon that a busy shop owner would not know.
4. Structure: each paragraph makes one point.
5. Risks stated: the post names at least one reason it might not work.
```

What changed: five qualities, each with a plain test for the score. A reviewer can now disagree with itself, and so can you.

### Pass 3: add a separate role and a severity scale

```text
Role: You are a reviewer who did not write this. You have only the draft and this rubric. You do not know what the author meant to say.

Severity for each finding:
- Blocker: the reader would be misled or would not act.
- Major: the post is weaker, and fixing it helps a lot.
- Minor: polish.
```

What changed: the reviewer has no access to the author's intentions, which are what the author was blind to. Findings now have an order.

### Pass 4: add the voice constraint and the line-citing check

```text
Constraints:
- Do not rewrite my text. Do not offer replacement paragraphs. Point at the problem and say what is missing.
- Keep my voice. Do not suggest changes of tone unless the rubric item is "Fit to reader".
- Cite every finding with the line number or the first five words of the sentence it refers to. A finding with no citation is dropped.

Final check, before you reply: count your findings, and for each, confirm it has a citation and a severity. If any lacks one, remove it and say so.
```

What changed: the review is anchored in the text, is told not to rewrite the author, and has to check itself before it answers.

### The final review brief

```text
Role: You are a reviewer who did not write this. You have only the draft and this brief.
Goal: The post should persuade a busy, sceptical small-shop owner to try a market stall.

Rubric (score 1 to 3 with a one-line reason):
1. Clear ask within the first three sentences.
2. Evidence: each claim has a reason, figure or example.
3. Fit to reader: no jargon.
4. Structure: one point per paragraph.
5. Risks stated: at least one reason it might not work.

Findings: list each as [Blocker|Major|Minor] + citation + what is wrong + what is missing.
Constraints: Do not rewrite. Do not offer replacement text. Keep my voice.
Check: every finding has a citation and a severity. Drop any that do not, and say so.
Draft:
[paste]
```

An example reply:

```text
Scores: 1 ask 2/3 (the ask arrives in line 6), 2 evidence 1/3 ("Stalls always sell well", line 9, has no figure), 3 fit 3/3, 4 structure 2/3 (lines 7 to 8 mix price, location and advice), 5 risks 1/3 (none named).
Findings:
[Major] line 9, "Stalls always sell well": a claim with no reason or figure. Missing: one example from Mina's own sales.
[Major] lines 7-8: price, location and advice share a paragraph. Missing: a split.
[Minor] line 3, "very, very small": the repeat adds nothing.
Check: 3 findings, each with a citation and a severity. None dropped.
```

Now check the check. Open the draft and read line 9. If the reviewer quoted it correctly, the review is anchored. If a line number points at the wrong sentence, the review did not read the text closely, and you can discount it. Replies vary between runs and tools.

**Run the review twice.** Open a second new chat, paste the same brief and draft, and compare the two reviews. A finding that appears in both is likely real. A finding that appears once deserves a second look. The 1 to 3 scores are labels, not measurements, so do not average them. If the two reviews disagree on a Blocker, tighten that rubric line.

:::note[Want consistency]
Keep the final brief in a notes app. Reuse it for every proposal, changing only the goal and the rubric lines.
:::

### Second pass: house style and facts

The first pass uses a general rubric. Real editing has levels: structural (does the piece work), copy (clarity, style and consistency) and proof (typos). Tell the assistant which level it does. Here it does a copy-level read against the house style, and it does not do the structural or proof levels.

A house-style rubric of four lines (an example, so use your own):

```text
1. Numerals: spell out one to nine, use figures from 10.
2. Attribution: every quote and every figure names its source in the same sentence.
3. Headline claim: the claim in the headline is supported in the body.
4. Sensitive wording: flag any wording that could defame a named person or business. Do not fix it.
```

Fact claims need a separate pass with source tracing. Ask the assistant to list every claim as a table. You fill in the source column, because the assistant must not supply sources:

| Line | Claim as written | Source (you fill this in) | Checked by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | Last spring I took a stall at the Saturday market in town | Sam's diary entry | Sam |
| 9 | Stalls always sell well | None found: needs a source or a rewrite | Sam |

Legal and sensitive review stay with a person.

## Under the bonnet

The vague ask failed because it gave the assistant no standard to measure against, so it reached for a pleasant average. Each pass adds a standard. The goal sets what good means, the rubric breaks it into testable parts, the role removes the author's intentions, and the severity scale sorts the findings.

Requiring a citation does three jobs. It forces the reviewer to point at real text. It makes findings checkable by you in seconds. And it exposes padding, because a finding about nothing cannot have a line number. A review that cites nothing is the same as the pleasant average in different clothes.

The voice constraint works as an [allowlist](../../glossary/#allowlist) for the reviewer's output: it may point, score and explain what is missing, and it may not write replacement text. Without it, many assistants rewrite your paragraph to be tidier and quietly remove what made it yours.

The checks that say the review is done: every finding has a citation and a severity, you checked at least one citation by hand, and the review was reviewed and approved by a named person who is not the preparer, or by the fallback reviewer in [using an assistant at work](../../journey/professional-guardrails/).

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
A rubric score from a model is a noisy measurement, which is why the main exercise runs the review twice. Do not average scores to make the noise look like a number.

Reviewer independence has limits, which [level 5](../../journey/05-check-the-work/) explains. Neither a fresh context nor a different model removes your rubric's own blind spots. Add a check by a human on a [sample](../../glossary/#sampling).

Hold the rubric still when comparing two drafts or two review briefs. If you edit the rubric after seeing a score you dislike, you have moved the ruler. Change the rubric in its own step, then re-score both.

An editorial team's version is a style guide with a second reader who has no stake in the piece.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: reviewing a draft.**

When asked to review, use only the draft and the rubric you are given. If the rubric is missing, ask for one. Ask once per task, in one message, with every open item and your default for each (at most five items). If nobody can answer, state your assumptions and carry on with read-only work. For any action that changes something, stop and report, and take no action.

Return findings with: severity, citation, what is wrong, what is missing. Drop any finding without a citation.

Do not rewrite the text. Do not propose replacement paragraphs. Keep the author's voice.

Before you reply, count your findings and confirm each has a citation and a severity. Report the count.

Check: the reply states the finding count, and each finding has a citation and a severity.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Asking the author's own chat to review.** The assistant remembers the intent and approves the work it just helped write. Fix: open a new chat, paste only the draft and the review brief.

**Accepting findings without checking one.** A confident finding can still point at the wrong sentence. Fix: check one citation by hand every time. If it is wrong, doubt the rest.

**Letting the review rewrite your voice.** The draft improves on paper and stops sounding like you. Fix: keep the "do not rewrite" constraint in the brief, and ask for what is missing, not for new text.

## Checkpoint

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. Agents may skip the quiz.</em></p>

You can now:

- Turn "check my work is good" into a goal, a rubric and a severity scale.
- Set up a reviewer who sees only the draft and the brief.
- Require citations, and use them to check the review.
- Keep the author's voice by forbidding rewrites.

**Self-test**

1. Why does the brief say the reviewer "does not know what the author meant to say"?
2. A review returns six findings and two have no citation. What does the brief tell the reviewer to do, and why is that useful?
3. You change the rubric after the first review, then the second draft scores higher. What can you say?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The author reads what they meant to write. A reviewer who knows the intent will fill the gaps the same way. A reviewer who lacks it reads what is on the page.
2. Drop them and say so. A finding without a citation cannot be checked, and it is often padding.
3. Very little. The rubric changed between the scores, so the comparison is not like for like. Score both drafts with the same rubric before you compare.

</details>
