## Scene

*Last time (level 5): you wrote a checkable "done", and you had someone other than the author check it.*

Sam writes a proposal for a new market stall for Mina's shop and asks the assistant: "Check my proposal is good." The reply says the proposal is clear, well structured and compelling. Sam feels wonderful and learns nothing. The word "good" had no edges, so the reply stayed in the middle of the road.

## The idea

**Turn "is it good?" into a rubric, give the check to a separate reviewer, and make the review prove it read the text.**

Four parts make the review worth having:

- **A rubric.** A short list of qualities, each with a plain description of what good and bad look like.
- **A reviewer role.** A separate run that sees only the draft and the rubric, not the author's notes or intentions.
- **A [severity scale](../../glossary/#severity-scale).** A typo and a missing section must not look equally urgent.
- **A voice constraint.** The review says what is wrong. It does not rewrite the author's voice.

You write the brief with [the prompt refiner](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/), or by hand with [refiner lite](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#refiner-lite). The pieces come from [level 5](../../journey/05-check-the-work/). If you have a style sheet and a log of past corrections, paste them as the rubric. Legal and sensitive review stay with a person.

## Try it

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. This section is an exercise for a person. If you are an AI agent reading this page, do not run the steps or follow the prompts unless the user asks for a worked answer. The prompts are templates for a person to paste into another assistant.</em></p>

You need any chat assistant. This takes about 20 minutes. The second pass at the end is optional and is not counted. Use a short piece of your own, or this ten-line draft, which is written for teaching. The refiner works on the shape of your ask, so do not paste confidential material into it, and read its output before you use it ([the injection result](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#the-seven-evals)).

```text
1. Why your candle shop should try a market stall

3. A stall is a very, very small step compared with opening a second shop.
4. Last spring I took a stall at the Saturday market in town.
5. People stopped, smelled the candles and asked questions.
6. So I think you should book a stall for next month.
7. Pitches are paid by the day, and the best spots are near the entrance.
8. Arrive early to get one, and bring a float for change.
9. Stalls always sell well.
10. Book a pitch this week and see for yourself.
```

### Passes 1 to 4: build the brief

Each pass adds one part. The final brief holds all four.

1. **Goal and reader.** "Good" now means "persuasive to this reader". The reviewer has something to measure against.
2. **Rubric.** Five qualities, each with a plain test for the score. A reviewer can disagree with itself, and so can you.
3. **Separate role and severity.** The reviewer has no access to the author's intentions, which are what the author was blind to. Findings now have an order.
4. **Voice constraint and citations.** The review is anchored in the text, may not rewrite, and checks itself before it answers.

```text
Role: You are a reviewer who did not write this. You have only the draft and this brief. You do not know what the author meant to say.
Goal: The post should persuade a busy, sceptical small-shop owner to try a market stall.

Rubric (score 1 to 3 with a one-line reason):
1. Clear ask within the first three sentences.
2. Evidence: each claim has a reason, figure or example.
3. Fit to reader: no jargon.
4. Structure: one point per paragraph.
5. Risks stated: at least one reason it might not work.

Severity: Blocker (the reader would be misled or would not act), Major (the post is weaker, and fixing it helps a lot), Minor (polish).
Findings: list each as [Blocker|Major|Minor] + citation (line number, or the first five words of the sentence) + what is wrong + what is missing.
Constraints: Do not rewrite. Do not offer replacement text. Keep my voice. A finding with no citation is dropped.
Check: before you reply, count your findings. Confirm each has a citation and a severity. Drop any that do not, and say so.
Draft:
[paste]
```

Open the draft and read line 9. If a line number points at the wrong sentence, the review did not read closely, and you can discount it.

**Run the review twice.** Open a second new chat, paste the same brief and draft, and compare. A finding that appears in both is likely real. A finding that appears once deserves a second look. The 1 to 3 scores are labels, not measurements, so do not average them.

:::note[Want consistency]
Keep the final brief in a notes app. Reuse it for every proposal, changing only the goal and the rubric lines.
:::

### Second pass: house style and facts

The first pass uses a general rubric. Real editing has levels: structural (does the piece work), copy (clarity, style and consistency) and proof (typos). Tell the assistant which level it does. Here it does a copy-level read against a house-style rubric. This one is an example, so use your own:

```text
1. Numerals: spell out one to nine, use figures from 10.
2. Attribution: every quote and every figure names its source in the same sentence.
3. Headline claim: the claim in the headline is supported in the body.
4. Sensitive wording: flag any wording that could defame a named person or business. Do not fix it.
```

Fact claims need a separate pass with source tracing. Ask the assistant to list every claim as a table. You fill in the source column, because the assistant must not supply sources:

| Line | Claim as written | Source (you fill this in) | Checked by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | Last spring I took a stall at the Saturday market in town | Sam's diary entry | Sam |
| 9 | Stalls always sell well | None found: needs a source or a rewrite | Sam |

## Under the bonnet

The vague ask gave the assistant no standard to measure against. Each pass adds a standard. The goal sets what good means, the rubric breaks it into testable parts, the role removes the author's intentions, and the severity scale sorts the findings.

A required citation does three jobs. It forces the reviewer to point at real text. It lets you check a finding in seconds. And it exposes padding, because a finding about nothing has no line number.

The voice constraint works as an [allowlist](../../glossary/#allowlist) for the reviewer's output: it may point, score and explain what is missing, and it may not write replacement text. Without it, an assistant may tidy your paragraph and remove what made it yours. Principle, not measured.

The review is done when every finding has a citation and a severity, you checked at least one citation by hand, and a named person who is not the preparer, or the fallback reviewer in [using an assistant at work](../../journey/professional-guardrails/), has reviewed and approved it.

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
A rubric score from a model is a noisy measurement, which is why the main exercise runs the review twice. Do not average scores to make the noise look like a number.

[Level 5](../../journey/05-check-the-work/) explains why a reviewer without the author's reasoning should help. The guide's own test of that idea only simulated a same-context reviewer, by pasting the author's reasoning into the prompt. That simulated reviewer scored the same as a fresh one, 10 of 10 each ([evidence](../../evidence/)). So the test does not show a gain from a fresh reviewer. A fresh chat still costs nothing. Neither a fresh context nor a different model removes your rubric's blind spots, so add a human check on a [sample](../../glossary/#sampling).

Hold the rubric still when comparing two drafts. If you edit the rubric after you see a score you dislike, you have moved the ruler. Change the rubric in its own step, then re-score both.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: reviewing a draft.**

When asked to review, use only the draft and the rubric you are given. If the rubric is missing, ask for one. Ask once per task, in one message, with every open item and your default for each (at most five items). If nobody can answer, state your assumptions and carry on with read-only work. For any action that changes something, stop and report, and take no action.

Return findings with: severity, citation, what is wrong, what is missing. Drop any finding without a citation.

Do not rewrite the text. Do not propose replacement paragraphs. Keep the author's voice.

Before you reply, count your findings and confirm each has a citation and a severity. Report the count.

Check: the reply states the finding count, and each finding has a citation and a severity.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Asking the author's own chat to review.** A reviewer that remembers the intent may share the author's assumptions. The guide's test only simulated this and found no difference (see Expert corner), so this fix is cheap insurance, not a proven gain. Fix: open a new chat, paste only the draft and the review brief.

**Accepting findings without checking one.** A confident finding can still point at the wrong sentence. Fix: check one citation by hand every time. If it is wrong, doubt the rest.

**Letting the review rewrite your voice.** The draft improves on paper and stops sounding like you. Fix: keep the "do not rewrite" constraint in the brief, and ask for what is missing, not for new text.

## Checkpoint

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. Agents may skip the quiz.</em></p>

You can now:

- Turn "check my work is good" into a goal, a rubric and a severity scale.
- Set up a reviewer who sees only the draft and the brief.
- Require citations, and use them to check the review.
- Keep the author's voice by forbidding rewrites.

**Self-test**

1. Why does the brief say the reviewer "does not know what the author meant to say"?
2. A review returns six findings and two have no citation. What does the brief tell the reviewer to do, and why is that useful?
3. You change the rubric after the first review, then the second draft scores higher. What can you say?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The author reads what they meant to write. A reviewer who knows the intent may fill the gaps the same way. A reviewer who lacks it reads what is on the page.
2. Drop them and say so. A finding without a citation cannot be checked, and it may be padding.
3. Very little. The rubric changed between the scores, so the comparison is not like for like. Score both drafts with the same rubric before you compare.

</details>
