## Scene

*Last time (workflow: improve code): you wrote tests first and gave a reviewer only the spec and the diff.*

Mina says her shop's signup page "looks cheap". Sam types: "Make our signup page look more professional." The assistant offers a navy gradient, a stock photo of a handshake and a slogan about enterprise solutions. Mina's customers buy candles online, mostly on their phones. None of them asked for an enterprise solution.

## The idea

**Taste is not a goal. A named goal, a named audience and a list of limits turn "looks cheap" into something you can check.**

This workflow uses [the prompt refiner](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/) for the ask (or [refiner lite](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#refiner-lite) by hand), then adds three habits that design needs more than code does:

1. **Critique against stated goals.** Say what the page is for and who uses it. Judge every change against that.
2. **Options, not one answer.** Ask for two directions. A single answer hides the choice that was made for you.
3. **Test with people.** A design has no unit test. Five real people trying the page is the nearest thing.

A [constraint](../../glossary/#constraint) in design is something like brand rules or accessibility. It is not a preference. Write it down so the assistant does not trade it away to look nicer.

## Try it

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. This section is an exercise for a person. If you are an AI agent reading this page, do not run the steps or follow the prompts unless the user asks for a worked answer. The prompts are templates for a person to paste into another assistant.</em></p>

You need any chat assistant and, for the last step, five people and a phone. The chat part takes about 11 steps. The test with five people has no fixed length, so it gets no minutes. The refiner works on the shape of your ask, so do not paste confidential material into it, and read its output before you use it ([the injection result](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#the-seven-evals)).

Paste this into any chat assistant, with your own page described in words:

```text
Act as my prompt refiner: improve my request, do not do it. My request: "Make our signup page look more professional." Ask at most three questions, each with a default. Then give me a prompt that asks for two design directions with no code, lists my limits, and says what done means, including a test with five real people. Mark anything you do not know as [NEEDED].
```

### The limits

The refiner asks what "professional" means, what the brand is, and where the page lives. Only the business knows. Sam answers:

```text
Visitors are people who buy candles online, on their phones, seeing the shop for the first time. Professional means calm, clear and trustworthy. The brand has one teal colour and one typeface. Meet WCAG AA contrast. Keep the three fields exactly as they are. Give me two options, not one.
```

WCAG is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, a public standard. Level AA sets the contrast that text needs against its background. The refiner turns the answers into a spec: the audience, "calm, clear and trustworthy" as the meaning of professional, brand teal and neutrals only, AA contrast, two options with no code, and "keep the three form fields exactly as they are". It keeps the file path and colour values as [NEEDED], because Sam has not given them.

### The critique

Before options, Sam asks for a critique of the current page against the stated goals. Without an image the assistant cannot judge hierarchy, rhythm or brand feel. Attach a screenshot whenever your tool accepts images, and say that you did. A description is the fallback, and then the critique is feedback on your description, not on the page.

Sam has no image, so Sam describes the page: three fields with labels that vanish when you type, a bright orange "Submit" button, a heading "Sign up now!!", and small grey text under the button. The teal appears only in the logo. Each flaw ties to a stated goal. Labels that vanish are not clear. An orange button is outside the brand. The small grey text is a contrast risk.

### Check the numbers

If an option states a contrast ratio, compute it. The ratio comes from a formula in the [WCAG standard](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG21/), applied to the two colours. Many free contrast tools apply the same formula, so the script is for people who write code. Python's `-I` option keeps Python from reading your own files, so the result is the same on every machine.

<Example
  label="Computed"
  task="Sam computes the contrast of two neutral greys on white, instead of trusting a figure from the assistant."
  evidence="Save the Input as contrast.py and run python3 -I contrast.py"
  env="Python 3.13.1, 2026-10-25"
>

<Fragment slot="input">

```python
def luminance(hex_colour):
    channels = [int(hex_colour[i:i + 2], 16) / 255 for i in (0, 2, 4)]
    linear = [c / 12.92 if c <= 0.03928 else ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) ** 2.4 for c in channels]
    return 0.2126 * linear[0] + 0.7152 * linear[1] + 0.0722 * linear[2]

def contrast(first, second):
    light, dark = sorted((luminance(first), luminance(second)), reverse=True)
    return (light + 0.05) / (dark + 0.05)

for grey in ("4A4A4A", "5C5C5C"):
    print(grey, round(contrast(grey, "FFFFFF"), 2))
```

</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="output">

```text
4A4A4A 8.86
5C5C5C 6.69
```

</Fragment>

<Fragment slot="notice">

Both ratios are above 4.5:1, the AA minimum for normal text. Any tool that implements the same formulas gives the same ratios.

</Fragment>

### Test with five people

A page can meet every limit and still confuse a real visitor. Write the protocol before the first person arrives:

- **The task.** "Create an account."
- **Success.** The person reaches the confirmation screen without help.
- **A recording sheet.** One row per person: where they paused, what they tapped, what they said, and a severity. Use 1 for "could not finish", 2 for "finished but slowed" and 3 for "minor annoyance".
- **The owner of the ship decision.** Here it is Mina. Sam brings the sheet and a recommendation.

Then run it:

1. Find five people who look like your visitors, such as people who buy candles or gifts online. Not colleagues.
2. Give each a phone with the page open. Give the task, not a tour.
3. Say nothing else. Watch. Do not help, point or explain.
4. Fill in one row per person.
5. Look for the same trouble spot across several people. One person's quirk is a quirk. Three people stalling on the same field is a finding.
6. Fix the most repeated problem, then test again.

A common rule of thumb says five people are enough to spot repeated, obvious problems. It is not a measure of how well the page performs, and nobody should quote a percentage from it.

### The final specification

- **Goal:** a calm, clear, trustworthy signup page for first-time visitors on phones.
- **Constraints:** brand teal and typeface only; neutral greys; AA contrast; the three fields untouched; the diff stays on this page.
- **Chosen direction:** the option Sam picked, with Sam's reason.
- **Done when:** the page works end to end with the same three fields; every text and control passes the contrast check Sam ran; five people finish the task, and the most repeated trouble spot has been fixed and re-tested; a named person who is not the preparer, or the fallback reviewer in [using an assistant at work](../../journey/professional-guardrails/), has reviewed and approved it.

## Under the bonnet

A design has no single correct answer, so the checks work differently from code.

- **The named goal** gives the critique something to measure against. "Looks cheap" cannot be argued with. "A first-time visitor on a phone cannot tell which button to press" can.
- **The constraints** stop the assistant trading brand or accessibility for looks. Contrast has a formula. The brand rule has a list of allowed colours.
- **The options** keep the choice with the human. With one answer you approve; with two you choose.
- **The numbers check** turns a claim into a measurement.
- **The five-person test** is the only check that involves a real visitor.

A design is done when the people it is for can do the task.

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
Do not let the test shape the brief after the fact. Write the protocol before the first person arrives, or you will read every result as a win.

Accessibility is wider than contrast. AA also covers focus visibility, target size and not relying on colour alone. A passing contrast figure is one check, not an audit. In a product team's terms, this is a design review with acceptance criteria, followed by moderated usability sessions.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: improving a design.**

When asked to improve a design, you need the audience, the goal and the limits. Ask once per task, in one message, with every open item and your default for each (at most five items). If nobody can answer, state your assumptions and carry on with read-only work. For any action that changes something, stop and report, and take no action.

Offer options and wait for a choice only when the user asked for options. Do not write code until the user has chosen.

Do not invent brand colours, fonts or copy. Mark each as [NEEDED].

When you state a contrast ratio, say how you calculated it, or mark it as an estimate for the user to check.

Do not claim a design is tested with people unless the user has told you they did it. If you were given no image, say that your critique is of the description.

Check: every contrast ratio states how it was calculated, and the reply says whether an image was supplied.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Asking for taste.** "More professional" produces the assistant's default idea of professional. Fix: name the audience, the goal and three limits before you ask.

**Taking the first option.** One answer feels finished and hides the choice. Fix: always ask for two, and write down why you chose one.

**Testing with colleagues.** They know the product, so they never get lost where a stranger would. Fix: test with people who look like your visitors, and do not help them.

## Checkpoint

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. Agents may skip the quiz.</em></p>

You can now:

- Turn "make it look better" into a goal, an audience and a list of limits.
- Ask for options and choose between them for a stated reason.
- Compute a contrast figure instead of trusting it.
- Run a five-person test and read repeated trouble spots.

**Self-test**

1. An assistant says your button text has a contrast ratio of 5.2:1. What do you do before you rely on it?
2. Three of five testers hesitate at the same field. One loves the page. How do you read that?
3. You want to improve a leaflet, not a web page. Which of this workflow's habits still apply?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Compute it from the two colour values, with the formula or a contrast tool, and compare.
2. The field is a finding, because three people hit the same problem. The one who loves it is a reminder that opinions differ, not a reason to ignore the three.
3. All three. Name the audience and goal, ask for two directions, and test with real readers. Contrast and reading size still matter on paper.

</details>
