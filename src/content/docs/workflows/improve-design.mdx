## Scene

Mina says her shop's signup page "looks cheap". Sam types: "Make our signup page look more professional." The assistant happily produces a navy-blue gradient, a stock photo of a handshake and the word "synergy". Mina's customers are people who sell candles at markets. None of them has ever said "synergy" out loud.

## The idea

**Taste is not a goal. A named goal, a named audience and a list of limits turn "looks cheap" into something you can check.**

This workflow uses [the prompt refiner](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/) for the ask, then adds three habits that design needs more than code does:

1. **Critique against stated goals.** Say what the page is for and who uses it. Judge every change against that.
2. **Options, not one answer.** Ask for two directions. A single answer hides the choice that was made for you.
3. **Test with people.** A design has no unit test. Five real people trying the page is the nearest thing.

A [constraint](../../glossary/#constraint) in design is something like brand rules or accessibility. It is not a preference. Write it down so the assistant cannot trade it away to look nicer.

## Try it

You need any chat assistant and, for the last step, five people and a phone. The replies below come from real runs on 2026-10-22. They are examples. Your tool will answer differently.

### Pass 0: the rough ask

```text
Make our signup page look more professional.
```

### Pass 1: the refiner asks about the goal

The refiner asked three questions, each with a default. In short:

- What does "professional" mean here? Do you have a reference or brand guide? Default: clean spacing and type, a restrained palette, consistent with the existing brand.
- Do you want the changes applied, or a review first? Default: recommendations first, with styling-only changes after.
- Where is the page, and what is it built with? Default: the assistant needs a path or address, so it is marked [NEEDED].

It also made a list of assumptions. "Professional" was read as visual design, not copy or the signup flow itself. It marked the page and its build as [NEEDED] and invented no colours or fonts.

What changed, in plain words: "professional" is still undefined, but the page, the brand and the audience are now named as missing. The refiner did not offer a design.

**Human checkpoint 1.** Sam answers what only the business knows, and sets the limits:

```text
Visitors are owners of small shops, on their phones, seeing us for the first time. Professional means calm, clear and trustworthy. The brand has one teal colour and one typeface. Meet WCAG AA contrast. Keep the three fields exactly as they are. Give me two options, not one.
```

WCAG is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, a public standard. Level AA sets the contrast that text needs against its background.

### Pass 2: the refiner turns answers into a spec

Its diff said, in part:

- **Added:** the audience in Context, so "mobile first" and "trustworthy to a first-time visitor" now drive the page.
- **Added:** "calm, clear and trustworthy" as the meaning of professional.
- **Added:** a rule to use only the brand teal, the brand typeface and neutrals.
- **Added:** WCAG AA contrast as a constraint and a done check.
- **Added:** a two-stage output: two options with no code, then wait for a choice.
- **Added:** guidance for when the teal cannot reach AA contrast, so the assistant does not quietly swap colours.
- **Changed:** "do not add, remove or rename form fields" became "keep the three form fields exactly as they are".

It kept the file path and the colour values as [NEEDED], because Sam had not given them.

### The critique

Before options, Sam asks for a critique of the current page against the stated goals. Sam describes the page in words, since there is no code in the chat: three fields with labels that vanish when you type, a bright orange "Submit" button, a heading "Sign up now!!", and a line of small grey text under the button. The teal appears only in the logo.

An example reply opened with what was missing, then listed corrections both options share:

```text
Both options share these corrections to the current page: labels moved above fields (not placeholders), orange button replaced with teal, 11px legal text raised to 14px, form fills the viewport width on mobile, heading rewritten.
```

Each of those ties back to a stated goal. Labels that vanish are not clear. An orange button is outside the brand. The small grey text is a contrast risk.

### The options

The assistant gave two directions, each under 150 words. In short:

- **Option A, "Quiet Form".** Calm through restraint. One column, lots of space, teal used only on the button and focus ring.
- **Option B, "Anchored Panel".** Trustworthy through structure. A teal band carries the brand, and the form sits on a white sheet below.

Each option listed layout, type, colour, states and copy. For each unknown it wrote [NEEDED]. For example, the contrast of teal on white could not be stated until Sam supplies the actual colour.

**Human checkpoint 2.** Sam reads both options against the goals. Sam does not pick the prettier one. Sam picks the one that best serves a first-time visitor on a phone, and notes why.

### Check the numbers

The options included contrast ratios for the neutral greys. Sam does not trust numbers from the assistant, so Sam calculates them. The contrast ratio comes from a formula in the WCAG standard, and any checker will compute it.

| Colour on white | The assistant said | Calculated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| #4A4A4A | 8.3:1 | 8.9:1 |
| #5C5C5C | 6.3:1 | 6.7:1 |

Both numbers pass AA, which needs 4.5:1 for normal text. The assistant's figures were wrong anyway. The lesson is not that the design fails. The lesson is that a number from an assistant is a claim to check, not a measurement.

### Test with five people

A page can meet every limit and still confuse a real visitor. So test it.

1. Find five people who look like your visitors. Small-shop owners, in this case. Not colleagues.
2. Give each a phone with the page open. Give a task, not a tour: "Create an account for your shop."
3. Say nothing else. Watch. Do not help, point or explain.
4. Write down where each person pauses, frowns, taps the wrong thing or asks a question.
5. Look for the same trouble spot across several people. One person's quirk is a quirk. Three people stalling on the same field is a finding.
6. Fix the most repeated problem, then test again.

Five people is enough to spot repeated, obvious problems. It is not a measurement of how well the page performs, and nobody should quote a percentage from it.

### The final specification

After the choice and the test, the spec has these parts:

- **Goal:** a calm, clear, trustworthy signup page for first-time visitors on phones.
- **Constraints:** brand teal and typeface only; neutral greys; AA contrast; the three fields untouched; the diff stays on this page.
- **Chosen direction:** the option Sam picked, with Sam's reason.
- **Done when:** the page works end to end with the same three fields; every text and control passes the contrast check Sam ran; five people finish the task, and the most repeated trouble spot has been fixed and re-tested.

### Try it yourself

Paste this into any chat assistant, with your own page described in words:

```text
Act as my prompt refiner: improve my request, do not do it. My request: "Make our signup page look more professional." Ask at most three questions, each with a default. Then give me a prompt that asks for two design directions with no code, lists my limits, and says what done means, including a test with five real people. Mark anything you do not know as [NEEDED].
```

## Under the bonnet

A design has no single correct answer, so the checks work differently from code.

- **The named goal** gives the critique something to measure against. "Looks cheap" cannot be argued with. "A first-time visitor on a phone cannot tell which button to press" can.
- **The constraints** stop the assistant trading brand or accessibility for looks. Contrast has a formula. The brand rule has a list of allowed colours.
- **The options** keep the choice with the human. With one answer you are approving; with two you are choosing.
- **The numbers check** catches claims the assistant made with confidence.
- **The five-person test** is the only check that involves a real visitor.

The checks that say when it is done are the ones in the final specification above. A design is done when the people it is for can do the task.

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
Assistants judge a design from a description, not from pixels, unless your tool can look at an image. A critique from words alone is a critique of your description. Attach a screenshot if your tool supports it, and say that you did.

Do not let the test shape the brief after the fact. Write the task and what counts as success before the first person arrives, or you will read every result as a win.

Accessibility is wider than contrast. AA also covers things like focus visibility, target size and not relying on colour alone. A passing contrast figure is one check, not an audit. In a product team's terms, this is a design review with acceptance criteria, followed by moderated usability sessions.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
When asked to improve a design, ask for the audience, the goal and the limits first. If you are not given them, state your assumptions at the top of the reply.

Offer at least two options when the user asks for options. Do not write code until the user has chosen.

Do not invent brand colours, fonts or copy. Mark each as [NEEDED].

When you state a contrast ratio, say how you calculated it, or mark it as an estimate for the user to check.

Do not claim a design is tested with people unless the user has told you they did it.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Asking for taste.** "More professional" produces the assistant's default idea of professional. Fix: name the audience, the goal and three limits before you ask.

**Taking the first option.** One answer feels finished and hides the choice. Fix: always ask for two, and write down why you chose one.

**Testing with colleagues.** They know the product, so they never get lost where a stranger would. Fix: test with people who look like your visitors, and do not help them.

## Checkpoint

You can now:

- Turn "make it look better" into a goal, an audience and a list of limits.
- Ask for options and choose between them for a stated reason.
- Check a contrast figure instead of trusting it.
- Run a five-person test and read repeated trouble spots.

**Self-test**

1. An assistant says your button text has a contrast ratio of 5.2:1. What do you do before you rely on it?
2. Three of five testers hesitate at the same field. One loves the page. How do you read that?
3. You want to improve a leaflet, not a web page. Which of this workflow's habits still apply?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Calculate it with a contrast checker from the two colour values. In the run above, the assistant's figures for two greys were off by a few tenths.
2. The field is a finding, because three people hit the same problem. The one who loves it is a reminder that opinions differ, not a reason to ignore the three.
3. All three. Name the audience and goal, ask for two directions, and test with real readers. Contrast and reading size still matter on paper.

</details>
