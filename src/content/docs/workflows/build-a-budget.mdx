## Scene

Sam types "Help me make a budget." Within a second the assistant produces a tidy table with rent at 1,200, groceries at 400 and "entertainment" at 150. They are lovely numbers. They are also nobody's numbers. Sam has not told it a single figure.

## The idea

**A budget is only as true as its inputs. The assistant must never supply a figure, only organise the ones you give it.**

Four rules do the work. Each one is written into the prompt, so the assistant sees them every time:

1. **Never invent a figure.** Every number comes from you, or is left blank and marked [NEEDED].
2. **Mark every assumption.** An assumed number is not a fact. The reply lists each one so you can strike it out.
3. **Find the lines that matter most.** Test how much the answer moves if the biggest items change. This is called sensitivity.
4. **Reconcile against reality.** Check the totals against a real bank or card statement before you trust the budget.

This workflow runs the rules twice: first for Sam's flat share, from [level 1](../../journey/01-say-it-properly/), then for Mina's shop. It uses [the prompt refiner](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/) to write the ask.

## Try it

You need any chat assistant and, for the shop, a few real figures of your own. The replies below come from real runs on 2026-10-22. They are examples. Your tool will answer differently.

### Part 1: the household

#### Pass 0: the rough ask

```text
Help me make a budget.
```

#### Pass 1: the refiner refuses to guess

The refiner asked three questions, each with a default: personal or something else; what the budget is for; and whether you have your real numbers ready or want a template. It listed its assumptions, including "currency, income, expenses and goals are all unknown and must come from you". Its prompt marked income, currency, fixed costs, spending and goals as [NEEDED]. It also added a constraint: "Use only the figures I give you. Don't fill in 'typical' amounts for anything I haven't supplied."

What changed, in plain words: "make a budget" became "organise my figures into a table", and the missing figures became visible blanks. It produced no budget.

One thing the refiner added that Sam did not ask for: up to five "observations" about where spending looks high. That is advice, and it is not what Sam wanted yet. A refiner can overreach too. Sam deletes that line.

**Human checkpoint 1.** Sam answers with what is true, and says what is not known:

```text
A household of three flatmates. Euros. Monthly. I want each person's monthly share of the shared bills. Last year's bills by month: Jan 212, Feb 198, Mar 187, Apr 164, May 151, Jun 142, Jul 139, Aug 141, Sep 158, Oct 183, Nov 205, Dec 240. I do not have the rent figure to hand.
```

#### Pass 2: the refiner narrows the job

The refiner saw that the answers described a smaller job than a full budget, and said so. Its diff said, in part:

- **Rewrote the Goal and Context.** The first prompt was a personal budget with savings targets. Your answers describe splitting shared bills three ways, so it rebuilt the prompt around that.
- **Kept rent as [NEEDED]** and told the assistant to leave a blank line for it instead of stalling.
- **Added your figures to Inputs verbatim.** It did not total or average them, because that is the task and not the prompt.
- **Tightened the Definition of done** to checks you can do with arithmetic.

It asked whether the split should be equal, and whether Sam wanted each month's actual share, a flat monthly amount, or both. Its default was both.

#### The final specification

```text
Goal: Work out each flatmate's monthly share of the shared bills, so the three of us know what to put in each month.
Inputs: Three people, equal split, euros. Twelve monthly totals, excluding rent (given). Monthly rent: [NEEDED].
Constraints: Use only the figures above. Round to whole euros and say that you did.
Output format: A table, one row per month. One flat monthly amount per person. A short note on the trade-off.
Definition of done: Every number traces to a figure given, or is a marked blank. The three shares add back to each month's total. The flat amount times twelve times three matches the year's total.
What to do if unsure: Say so and leave a marked blank. Do not guess.
```

Sam changes one line. The draft said "round to whole euros", but the flatmates pay by bank transfer, so Sam asks for "round to the cent, and say how you handled any leftover cent". This is the kind of edit only the person can make.

#### The reconciliation check

The final spec says the shares must add back to the total. Sam checks, with arithmetic and not by trusting the assistant.

- The twelve bills add to 2,120.
- One flatmate's exact yearly share is 2,120 divided by three, which is 706.67 to the cent.
- Add the twelve rounded monthly shares for one person. The result is 706.66. One cent short.
- Three people at 706.66 make 2,119.98. Two cents short of the bills.
- A flat monthly amount of 58.89 per person comes to 2,120.04 across three people for twelve months. Four cents over.

So the "add back" check fails by a few cents, and nobody made a mistake. Rounding to the cent loses or gains fractions. The fix is a rule, not a better assistant: "One flatmate pays the extra cent in the month with the largest remainder, and the reply says which." Level 1 asked for the same thing: say how a spare cent was handled.

This is the February slip's cousin. An assistant that wants the table to "add up" can nudge a number quietly. A check that you run yourself will catch it.

Last, Sam compares the table with the real bank statements. The step is the same in every budget: take the statement for each month, find the lines that make up the bills, and confirm the table matches. A mismatch is a finding. It is either a bill that was left out or a figure Sam mis-typed.

### Part 2: the shop

Mina asks Sam to do the same for her candle shop. The shop has more lines and more at stake, so the four rules get stricter.

#### The ask

```text
Goal: Show Mina's monthly profit for her candle shop and which costs it depends on most.
Inputs: Sales and costs by month for the last six months, pasted from Mina's bank statements below. Anything not on a statement is [NEEDED].
Constraints: Use only figures from the statements. Never estimate a missing figure. List every assumption on its own line. Say which lines you could not find on a statement.
Output format: A table of months by line item. Then the three biggest cost lines and what happens to profit if each moves up by 10%. Then the sales line and what happens if it moves down by 10%.
Definition of done: Each total ties to the statements. Differences are listed with amounts. Every assumption is marked.
What to do if unsure: Leave a blank and mark it. Do not guess.
```

Sam checks this against the rules, then runs it through the refiner to find gaps before pasting statements.

#### Sensitivity: which line matters most

Sensitivity shows how much the answer moves when one input moves. Here is a tiny example with made-up round numbers, invented to show the method. They are not Mina's figures.

| Line | Monthly amount |
| --- | --- |
| Sales | 3,000 |
| Materials | 900 |
| Postage | 450 |
| Stall fees | 300 |
| Platform fees | 120 |
| Everything else | 330 |
| **Profit** | **900** |

Costs add to 2,100, and 3,000 minus 2,100 is 900. Now move one line at a time by 10%, and keep the others fixed.

- Materials up 10% is 90 more. Profit falls to 810.
- Sales down 10% is 300 less. Profit falls to 600.

The same 10% move costs 90 on materials and 300 on sales. Profit is far more sensitive to sales than to materials, so a weak month of sales is the thing to watch. The example assumes costs stay the same when sales fall. That is rarely true, because materials fall with sales. Write it down as an assumption, and let Mina correct it.

#### Reconcile the shop

For the shop, the reconciliation has three steps:

1. Add the budget's sales for each month. Compare with the deposits on the statement. They must match, or the difference must be named.
2. Add the budget's costs. Compare with the payments on the statement.
3. List every line on the statement that the budget does not mention. An unmentioned payment is a hidden cost.

**Human checkpoint 2.** Sam sends Mina the list of differences and assumptions, not just the table. Mina decides what each difference is. The assistant cannot know whether an unexplained payment was a stall deposit or a personal purchase.

### Try it yourself

Paste this into any chat assistant, and add your own figures:

```text
Act as my prompt refiner: improve my request, do not do it. My request: "Help me make a budget." Ask at most three questions, each with a default. Then give me a prompt that never invents a figure, marks every assumption, tests the three biggest lines by 10%, and ends with a check against my bank statement. Mark anything you do not know as [NEEDED].
```

Then give it your real figures, check every total by hand, and compare with a statement.

## Under the bonnet

Why each rule is there:

- **No invented figures.** An assistant fills a gap with a likely number. A likely number in a budget looks exactly like a true one.
- **Marked assumptions.** A guess is fine when you can see it. You can strike it out or replace it.
- **Sensitivity.** The biggest line is not always the most important one. Moving each line the same amount shows which one drives the answer.
- **Reconciliation.** A budget that adds up inside itself can still be wrong. The statement is the outside evidence.

The checks that say when it is done:

- Every number traces to a figure you gave, or is a marked blank.
- Rounding differences are named and handled by a rule.
- The totals tie to the statements, and every difference has an explanation.
- The assumptions are listed and the person who owns the money has read them.

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
Assistants add up badly. They write sums that look right. Do arithmetic in a spreadsheet or a calculator tool, and have the assistant show its working only to read it. [Level 5](../../journey/05-check-the-work/) covers independent checks.

Sensitivity by moving each line 10% is a quick screen. It is not a forecast. It ignores that lines move together, and that a 10% change is not equally likely for every line. The choice of 10% here is for illustration. Pick a range you can defend for each line.

This is money, so keep data to what the job needs. Paste the lines you need, not the whole statement. Remove account numbers and names of people. In a finance team's terms, this is a bank reconciliation with a variance explanation, run before the numbers are signed off.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
Never supply a financial figure. Use only figures the user gave you. Mark each missing figure as [NEEDED: what is missing].

List every assumption on its own line.

State how you handled rounding, and which line absorbed any remainder.

Do not do arithmetic in prose and call it checked. Show the inputs and the result so the user can verify, or use a calculator tool if you have one.

When asked for advice, ask whether the user wants advice. Do not add it by default.

After building a table, list what you could not reconcile.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Letting the assistant fill the gaps.** It writes plausible figures, and they end up in the budget. Fix: say "use only my figures" in the prompt, and search the reply for any number you did not give.

**Trusting a total that adds up.** A table can add up and still be wrong, because the inputs were wrong. Fix: compare with a real statement.

**Forgetting what the 10% test assumed.** Sales fall and materials fall with them, but the test held them fixed. Fix: list that as an assumption, and let the owner correct it.

## Checkpoint

You can now:

- Write a budget ask that forbids invented figures.
- Spot and list assumptions in a reply.
- Find which line moves the answer most.
- Reconcile a budget with a statement and explain each difference.

**Self-test**

1. The reply shows "Insurance: 85". You never gave an insurance figure. What do you do?
2. Twelve rounded monthly shares add to 706.66, and the exact share is 706.67. Is the assistant wrong?
3. A budget's totals match the statements except for one payment of unknown purpose. What happens next?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Treat it as an invented figure. Remove it, mark it [NEEDED], and add a line to the prompt that forbids estimates.
