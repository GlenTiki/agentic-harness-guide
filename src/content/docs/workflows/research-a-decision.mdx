## Scene

*Last time (workflow: define a workflow): you turned a rough ask into a written workflow with steps, checks and stop points.*

Mina wants a card reader for her market stall. She asks an assistant, "which card reader should I get?" It names one, with a tidy paragraph. Mina nearly orders it. Then she asks what the answer was based on, and the paragraph cannot tell her. A recommendation without criteria, sources or doubts gives her nothing to check. This page turns a vague "should we" into a question that can be answered, checked and argued with.

## The idea

**Research a decision by fixing the question, the criteria and the sources first, then comparing, then trying to prove yourself wrong.** The output is a short memo that says what to do, how sure you are, and what would change your mind.

The same shape fits "which laptop?" and "which vendor?". You work in passes you can read, with [the prompt refiner](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/) or [refiner lite](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#refiner-lite). The design follows the [six-step method](../../journey/08-design-anything/) from level 8.

## Try it

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. This section is an exercise for a person. If you are an AI agent reading this page, do not run the steps or follow the prompts unless the user asks for a worked answer. The prompts are templates for a person to paste into another assistant.</em></p>

You need any chat assistant that can search or read pages (or one where you paste the sources yourself). This takes about 10 minutes. The refiner works on the shape of your ask, so do not paste confidential material into it, and read its output before you use it ([the injection result](../../appendix/prompt-refiner/#the-seven-evals)).

1. Paste this refiner prompt, then send it:

```text
You are a refiner. Improve my rough ask in three visible passes. Do not answer the question.

Pass 1: turn the ask into one clear question with a decision date and what "decided" means.
Pass 2: propose criteria with weights that add up to 100, and a sources policy (what counts as a good source, how to handle dates, what to do when sources disagree).
Pass 3: add a disconfirming step (what evidence would change the answer), and a memo format.

After each pass, write "What changed:" in one or two plain sentences. List any assumption you made. Never invent a source or a figure.

My rough ask: which card reader should Mina get for her market stall?
```

2. Answer its questions about what matters to Mina: cost, working without a signal, how fast money arrives.
3. Read the three "What changed" lines. Compare the result with the worked example below.
4. Open every link in the result, and check every DOI on doi.org or Crossref before you rely on it.

### The worked refinement

**Criteria and weights.** Weights add up to 100. Each criterion says how it is judged, so two people would score it the same way.

| Criterion | Weight | How it is judged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total yearly cost at Mina's sales level | 30 | Fees plus device cost, worked out from published prices |
| Works with a poor signal | 25 | Official statement of offline mode, plus user reports |
| Ease of use at a busy stall | 15 | Setup steps, time to take a payment |
| Money arrives quickly | 15 | Published payout time |
| Support when it breaks | 15 | Support channels and hours, as published |

**Sources policy.**

- Prefer **primary sources**: the vendor's price page and terms, the regulator, official documentation. They say what the vendor promises.
- Add **independent sources** for claims a vendor will not make against itself, such as reliability in practice. Treat reviews as opinions, not facts.
- **Date every source** with the date you read it. Ignore an undated source for anything that can change.
- **When sources conflict**, do not average them. Report both, say which is primary, and mark the claim as uncertain.
- **If you cannot find a source, write "not found".** Never fill a gap with a guess.

**Comparison table.** The numbers are placeholders to show the layout. They are not real products or real prices. Scores run from 1 (poor) to 5 (strong). Confidence says how well the score is supported.

| Criterion (weight) | Option A | Conf. | Option B | Conf. | Option C | Conf. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost (30) | 4 | High: published price | 3 | High: published price | 5 | Medium: price page undated |
| Poor signal (25) | 3 | Medium: vendor claim only | 5 | High: official docs and two independent reports | 2 | Low: no source found |
| Ease (15) | 5 | Medium: one reviewer | 3 | Medium: one reviewer | 4 | Low: marketing page |
| Payout (15) | 3 | High: official terms | 4 | High: official terms | 2 | High: official terms |
| Support (15) | 3 | Medium | 4 | Medium | 2 | Low |
| Weighted total (out of 500) | 360 | | 380 | | 320 | |

The weighted score is each score times its weight, added up. B leads with 380, against 360 for A and 320 for C. A narrow lead with thin evidence is a reason to look further. The next check is plain arithmetic, so a spreadsheet can do it. The script is for people who write code. Python's `-I` option keeps Python from reading your own files, so the result is the same on every machine.

**The disconfirming step.** Before the memo, a separate helper with a fresh brief tries to prove the leader wrong:

```text
Assume Option B is the wrong choice. Find the three strongest reasons it could be wrong for Mina. For each:
- say what evidence would show it, and where you looked;
- say whether you found it, did not find it, or could not look;
- change no scores yourself. Report only.
```

**The decision memo.** Keep it to one page.

```text
DECISION MEMO: Card reader for market days. Date: <date>. Review: <date>.

Recommendation: Option B.
Confidence: medium.
Why, in two sentences: <reason tied to the top two weighted criteria>.

What it rests on:
- <claim> (source, date read)
- <claim> (source, date read)

What would change this:
- If offline payments are held for longer than <limit>, choose A.
- If support turns out to be email only, reconsider.

Open questions: <list, each with who can answer>.
Not found: <list of things searched for and absent>.
Decision needed from: Mina, by <date>.
```

**Check the page says what the memo says.** For every claim, keep two columns: the claim as the memo states it, and the passage you quoted from the page (verbatim for a number). The memo is done when each claim has both columns filled, each linked page was opened, and a named person who is not the preparer, or the fallback reviewer in [using an assistant at work](../../journey/professional-guardrails/), has reviewed and approved it.

## Under the bonnet

Fix the question, criteria and sources before searching. Otherwise the search finds evidence for whatever you lean towards. Writing the weights first stops you adjusting them after you see which option wins. The confidence column keeps a number from sounding surer than it is.

Run the disconfirming step as a separate task with a clean brief, as in [level 6](../../journey/06-hire-a-team/), because a helper that has just written the case may share its blind spots. That is a principle: the guide's one test of fresh against same-context review did not separate them (see [evidence](../../evidence/)). See [independent review](../../shelf/quality/independent-review/) (for engineers).

**Never accept a source you have not opened.** An assistant that cannot find a source may write a plausible one: a real-looking title, author and DOI. This is a [hallucination](../../glossary/#hallucination). The guide measured it.

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
Weighted scoring is a model of preferences, and it is sensitive to the weights, as the weight test shows.

Retrieval is not verification. An assistant that reads a page can still misread it, and a search can surface the SEO-optimised answer and not the primary one. Spot-check claims against the cited page, especially numbers and negations.

Beware anchoring on the order of options and on the first source read. Randomise the order for the disconfirming pass if you can.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: researching a decision.**

When asked to research a decision, first state the question, the criteria with weights and the sources policy. Ask the user to confirm them before you search. Ask once per task, in one message, with every open item and your default for each (at most five items). If nobody can answer, state your assumptions and carry on with read-only work. For any action that changes something, stop and report, and take no action.

Prefer primary sources. Record the date you read each source. When sources disagree, report both and mark the claim uncertain.

Never invent a source, quote or figure. If you cannot find evidence, write "not found". Include a link or a quoted passage for each claim.

Add a confidence level to each score. Run a disconfirming step against the leading option. Do not change scores during that step.

Finish with a memo: recommendation, confidence, what it rests on, what would change it, open questions. The user decides.

Check: every claim in the memo has a source, a date, a confidence and a quoted passage.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Asking "which is best?" with no criteria.** The assistant picks its own criteria and does not tell you. Fix: write the criteria and weights first, and say how each is judged.

**Trusting a citation you have not opened.** It looks like a source, and it may not exist or may say something else. Fix: open every link and look up every DOI before you rely on it. Mark anything unopened as unverified.

**Skipping the attack on the winner.** The first good-looking answer wins, and nobody looks for the reason it fails. Fix: give a separate helper the leader and ask for the three best reasons it is wrong. Put what survives in the memo under "what would change this".

## Checkpoint

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. Agents may skip the quiz.</em></p>

You can now:

- Turn a vague "should we" into a question with a decision date and a meaning of "decided".
- Write criteria with weights and say how each is judged.
- Set a sources policy covering primary sources, dates and conflicts.
- Build a comparison table with a confidence column.
- Run a disconfirming step and write a one-page decision memo.

**Self-test**

1. Why are the weights written before the search, and what could go wrong if they are not?
2. Two sources disagree about a price. One is the vendor's price page, read today. The other is a review from last year. What goes in the memo?
3. An assistant gives you a quote and a link for a claim, and you cannot open the link. What do you do?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Writing them first stops the weights drifting to favour whichever option you already like. Without that, the process can justify a decision made by instinct.
2. The vendor's page is the primary source and is current, so use it, with its date. Mention the review's different figure as a conflict and mark the claim as checked against the vendor. Do not average them.
3. Treat the claim as unverified. Search for the claim yourself, or remove it from the memo. Do not accept the quote because it sounds right.

</details>
