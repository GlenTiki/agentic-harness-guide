---
title: AI assistant guardrails at work
description: Learn what to keep out of an assistant, who signs off, how much to check, what to do when it goes wrong and what evidence to keep.
---

This page is for anyone who uses an assistant for work that carries their name, such as finance, legal, HR or consulting. It is not legal, tax or regulatory advice and does not replace your organisation's AI policy. Read it after [level 0](../00-hello-robot/) and again before you connect real data.

## The short version

1. Never paste client names, account numbers, payroll or tax data, bank logins or unreleased results.
2. You sign the work. The assistant drafts it. A named person who did not prepare it reviews it, or you use the fallback reviewer below.
3. Check figures with a formula or an independent source. Do not ask the same assistant to check itself.
4. Read every output until you have 30 clean ones in a row. After that, sample. Never sample totals or anything signed.
5. Keep one short note per piece of work: what you did, what you checked, who prepared it and who reviewed it.

If you work in finance, [the finance section](#if-you-work-in-finance) adds the thresholds and the working paper.

## You stay responsible

You sign the work. The assistant drafts it. Treat it like a fast junior who writes well and can get facts wrong. In one test, at least one DOI did not resolve in 16 of 20 trials of claude-sonnet-5-5 on 2026-10-25 (see [the evidence page](../../evidence/)). You read what it produces and the result is yours.

Your organisation's AI policy outranks this page. If there is no policy, ask before you use real client data.

## Before you paste anything

Check what the tool keeps and whether it trains on what you send. Consumer and business plans differ. Ask your IT team. If you have none, use the small-firm default below.

Check four things before you paste real client data:

- **A data processing agreement.** A [data processing agreement](../../glossary/#data-processing-agreement) is the contract that says how the provider may use your data. Ask whether the provider offers one on your plan.
- **Client consent and engagement terms.** Read the engagement letter. It may limit who can see the client's data.
- **Personal-data law.** Payroll rows are personal data. Find the rule that applies in your country.
- **Your professional body.** Look for its guidance on using an assistant.

| Never paste | Redact first | Fine to paste |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client names | Names in notes: swap for codes | Example figures from this guide |
| Account numbers and IBANs (international bank account numbers) | Amounts: round them if the task allows | Your own published writing |
| Payroll and employee data | Email addresses and phone numbers | A template with blanks |
| Tax references | Dates that identify a person | Public rules and guidance |
| Bank logins and access keys | | |
| Unreleased results | | |

### Small-firm default

If you have no IT team, use these settings as your minimum:

- A business or team plan, not a personal one.
- Training on your data switched off. Check the setting after every plan change.
- The retention period known. Read it, and write it in your note.
- No client identifiers in the chat. Anonymise first, or use synthetic data.

### Where the work is filed

File the working paper and the exported chat in the firm's document system, not on a personal drive. The provider also holds a copy. Check how long it keeps it, and delete the chat if your plan allows.

## How to anonymise a spreadsheet

1. Replace names with codes, such as `Client A`, `Supplier 3`.
2. Round or scramble amounts when the task allows. A formula check does not need the true figures.
3. Keep the key that maps codes back to names offline, not in the chat.
4. Read the result back and look for anything that still identifies a person or a client.

## One chat per client

Use a separate chat or project for each client. Turn memory off when the data identifies a client. Each extra tool or model is another processor of the data, so ask what it receives before you connect it.

## Who signs off

Name three roles for any piece of work:

- **Preparer.** The person who produces the work with the assistant.
- **Reviewer.** A different person who checks it. A person never reviews their own work, except through the fallback below.
- **Approver.** The person who accepts it and carries the decision.

The assistant is none of these. A [reviewer assistant](../05-check-the-work/) can filter errors for a human reviewer but does not replace one.

### Fallback reviewer

If you work alone, no second person exists. Use a fallback reviewer. It is weaker than a second person, and you should say so in your note. Do all of these:

1. Re-read the work on the next working day against the written definition of done.
2. Rerun the formulas from scratch, not from the saved results.
3. Check the first ten per cent and the last ten per cent of the items.
4. For figures that matter, swap work with a peer, use a different tool, or ask an outside accountant.

## What you never delegate

You may delegate drafting, extracting, writing formulas and first-pass checking.

Never delegate:

- Final numbers.
- Accounting or legal treatments.
- Advice to a client.
- Anything that carries your name.
- Approving a payment.
- Changing bank details.

The assistant may draft, calculate with a formula you can see, and flag. It does not decide these. Accepting a reconciling item and signing off are also yours.

## Checking and sampling

Read every output until you have 30 clean outputs in a row. After that, read a random 5 items from each batch and keep the full read for anything that leaves your control. Return to a full read when the tool, the model, the prompt or the input type changes, or when one sampled item is wrong. Never sample final totals, reconciling items, or anything signed.

Thirty clean runs in a row means the failure rate is probably below about 1 in 10 (the rule of three: 3 divided by 30; exact upper bound 9.5% at 95% confidence). That is a loose bound. A 5-item sample from a batch with a 10% fault rate catches it only 41% of the time in one batch and 88% over four batches. A sample tells you whether the process is still working. It does not clear any single item.

An auditor tests a control on a sample and decides how far to rely on it. Treat the assistant the same way: [sampling](../../glossary/#sampling) tells you how far you can rely on its output, and a change in inputs or tools means you read everything again. [Level 7](../07-run-it-for-real/) applies the rule to a many-step job.

## If you work in finance

### Tolerance and investigation threshold

Set a [tolerance](../../glossary/#tolerance) and an [investigation threshold](../../glossary/#investigation-threshold) before the run, not after you see the result. Here is a starting point to adapt (illustrative):

- A reconciliation clears to 0.00 with every item named and traced to a source. A rounding difference of up to 1.00 is allowed only as a named rounding item that the approver initials. Any other difference stops the close.
- A line more than 5% and more than 50.00 from budget gets an explanation from the owner. The "and" is an example. Set your own rule.

### Keep evidence

A chat transcript is not a [working paper](../../glossary/#working-paper) unless you export it and file it. Keep a short note for each piece of work with these ten fields:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | Why you did the work |
| Source and reference | Where the data came from, and its reference |
| Prompt | The prompt, or its saved version |
| Tool and version | The product and the version you used |
| Date | The date of the run |
| Output | The output you relied on |
| Checks performed | Each check, and its result |
| Exceptions | Anything that failed or you overrode |
| Preparer | Initials and date |
| Reviewer | Initials and date |

### If you are asked

If a manager, a client or an auditor asks, you can say four things:

1. What you did, and with which tool.
2. What you checked.
3. What you agreed to source.
4. That the output is a draft until a named reviewer has reviewed it.

Your note gives each answer.

## When it goes wrong

An error made with an assistant can reach a client or a filing. When it does:

1. Stop using the batch.
2. Find which outputs shared the cause: the same prompt, the same model or the same kind of input.
3. Correct the error and tell the people affected under your normal error policy.
4. Log the exception in the working paper.
5. Add a seeded test for it: a case that reproduces the error, so the check can catch it next time.

Whether to tell a client or your employer that you used an assistant depends on your policy and client terms. Where there is none, ask.

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: work with real data.**

Before any action that changes something outside your working files, ask. The full rule is in [level 4](../04-trust-but-verify/).

Do not copy a secret into output, logs, memory or a tool call that sends it elsewhere. If a task needs a secret, ask the user to supply it through an approved connector. If one appears in text you read, do not repeat it and tell the user where it is.

Check: no secret appears in any output, and every change has a yes recorded in the user's chat message or a ledger line before it.
