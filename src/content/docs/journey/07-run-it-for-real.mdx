## Scene

*Last time: you split Mina's month-end into Reader, Calculator, Reviewer and Reporter helpers, each with a bounded brief. If you arrive here directly: Sam does the books for Mina's candle shop. The same slip comes back in several levels: a month counted twice, or a month missing.*

*Only use a chat window? You can skip to [level 8](../08-design-anything/) and come back when you run jobs for real.*

A restaurant kitchen at the Saturday rush has no time for heroics. Every order ticket is pinned up. Every dish passes the pass, where someone looks at it before it leaves. When the fryer breaks, a person calls it out loud, and nobody keeps cooking chips into a cold fryer. A good kitchen is not one where nothing goes wrong. It is one where you can see what went wrong, and where a problem stays small. Mina's month-end run works on Tuesday. On Friday it does something odd, and nobody can say what.

## The idea

**A job with many steps fails more often than any one of its steps, so you measure it, record it and put limits on it.** Three ideas turn a clever demo into something you can leave alone:

- **Reliability maths** tells you how often a many-step job succeeds, and which step to fix first.
- **An [audit log](../../glossary/#audit-log)** is a written record of every step, so you can see what happened afterwards.
- **Limits** cap time, spend and retries, so a bad run stays small.

### Why many steps hurt

Roll ten twenty-sided dice. A 1 on any die counts as a failure. Each die is fine 19 times in 20, which is 95%. About six rolls in ten show no 1 at all. That is a job of ten steps, each right 95 times in 100: about six runs in ten come out fully right. This is **[compounding error](../../glossary/#compounding-error)**: small error rates multiply along a chain. In a spreadsheet, type `=0.95^10` to check. It gives 0.599.

In plain words: one weak step drags the whole job down. The table below uses made-up numbers to show the shape of the effect.

*Illustrative numbers, made up for teaching. They are not measurements of any real system.*

| Case | Setup | Out of 100 runs fully right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Ten steps, each right 95 in 100 | 60 (59.9) |
| B | Nine steps at 95 in 100, one step at 80 in 100 | 50 (50.4) |

Case B has one weak step, and about ten runs in a hundred are lost for it. The arithmetic for B: nine steps at 95% give 0.95^9 = 0.630, and the weak step brings that to 0.630 x 0.80 = 0.504. Treat these as the best case: a wrong source file feeding every step breaks the multiplication, because the steps are no longer independent.

A check on the weak step, and a retry, wins runs back. The full table is in the first Go deeper block under "Under the bonnet".

To get real numbers, run the job many times on cases where you know the answer, and count. [Evals](../../glossary/#eval) from level 5 are the tool. Count per step if you can, so you know which one is weak.

### The audit log

When the run goes odd on Friday, you need to look at what each step did. An audit log is a plain list of events, one per step, written as the step happens. Here is the shape a person could keep in a notes app. It comes from Mina's September run on 2 October 2026, with times in UTC.

| Time | Step | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 09:14:03 | read | Read four files. Nine months, all different. |
| 09:14:12 | calculate | Joined with refunds. Ten months, total 48,800.00. |
| 09:14:14 | check | Failed: repeated month, February. |
| 09:14:14 | stop | Held. Waiting for a person. |

These steps match the helpers from level 6: read is the Reader, calculate is the Calculator, and check is the Reviewer.

Software writes the same record as one line per step, with the time first and one [run identity](../../glossary/#run-identity) (a string made once at the start) on every line:

```text
t=2026-10-02T09:14:03Z run=2026-10-02-0412 step=read      status=ok   files=4 rows=9 months=9 secs=3 cost=0.01
t=2026-10-02T09:14:12Z run=2026-10-02-0412 step=calculate status=ok   months=10 total=48800.00 secs=9 cost=0.04
t=2026-10-02T09:14:14Z run=2026-10-02-0412 step=check     status=FAIL reason="repeated month: Feb" secs=2 cost=0.01
t=2026-10-02T09:14:14Z run=2026-10-02-0412 step=stop      status=held waiting_for=human
```

Automation tools show something similar as a run history: a list of runs and the steps in each. Now you can trace the February slip. The read step found nine months. The calculate step reports ten, and the total of 48,800.00 is 4,200.00 too high. The check failed, and the run stopped there. The duplicate was produced at "calculate", not "read". That is a different fix from re-exporting the data.

### Limits

A run with no limits can run forever or spend without bound. Set these before you leave it alone:

- **Time cap.** A step that takes longer than a set time, say 120 seconds, is treated as failed.
- **[Spend cap](../../glossary/#spend-cap).** A run stops when it has used its budget of paid usage.
- **Step cap.** A run stops after a fixed number of steps, so a loop cannot go round forever.
- **Bounded [retries](../../glossary/#retry).** A failed step may retry at most twice (three attempts in all), with a pause that grows. Then it stops and reports.

:::tip[New to this]
A **retry** helps when the failure was a hiccup, such as a slow network. It does not help when the failure is a wrong answer, because the same wrong answer often comes back. That is why you cap retries and add a check.
:::

## Try it

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. This section is an exercise for a person. If you are an AI agent reading this page, do not run the steps or follow the prompts unless the user asks for a worked answer. The prompts are templates for a person to paste into another assistant.</em></p>

You need any chat assistant and, ideally, a spreadsheet. This takes about 10 minutes. Phone OK. No special tools are needed.

1. Work out the arithmetic yourself. A job has eight steps. Seven are 97% right. One is 85% right. Calculate the chance of a fully right run. Paper arithmetic is optional: in a spreadsheet, type `=0.97^7*0.85`. The answer is 68.7%.
2. Ask an assistant to check your working and then explore the check. Paste this:

```text
A job has eight steps in a row. Seven steps are each right 97 percent of the time. One step is right 85 percent of the time. All eight must be right for the run to be right.

1. Show the arithmetic for the chance that a run is fully right.
2. Now add a check on the weak step that catches 90 percent of its errors and retries successfully. Show the new chance.
3. Say in one sentence which change helped more: fixing the weak step to 95 percent, or adding the check. Show the arithmetic for both.

These numbers are made up. Do not claim they describe any real system.
```

An example reply:

```text
1. Seven steps: 0.97^7 = 0.808. Times 0.85 = 0.687, so about 68.7 percent.
2. Weak step becomes 0.85 + (0.15 x 0.90) = 0.985. 0.808 x 0.985 = 0.796, so about 79.6 percent.
3. Fixing the weak step to 0.95 gives 0.808 x 0.95 = 0.768, about 76.8 percent. The check helps more here (79.6 versus 76.8).
```

3. Check each number against your own working. Did the assistant get the arithmetic right? It is a good habit to compute these yourself.
4. Now write down your own audit log for a three-step job of your choice, as a list with three columns: time, step, what happened. Give each step a name, a status and one detail.

Tick it off: add the outcomes you can now do on [your progress page](../progress/).

## Under the bonnet

*How it works. Optional reading.*

Reliability maths only works if steps are independent, which they rarely are. If the first step reads the wrong file, every later step is wrong in the same direction. Errors can also cancel, so the real number may be higher or lower than the multiplied one. Treat the multiplication as a rough guide to which part of the chain is fragile, then measure.

An audit log is useful only if it is written as the step happens, not reconstructed afterwards. It records what the step was asked, what it returned in short form, whether it passed its check, how long it took, and what it cost. It does not need to hold the full text of every document. Keep personal data out of it. The log should let a stranger answer "what happened in run 0412?" without opening the original chat. For engineers: the shelf page on [cost and resources](../../shelf/operations/cost-and-resources/).

<details class="go-deeper">
<summary>Go deeper: the full reliability table (cases C and D)</summary>

In plain words: a check on the weak step wins back most of the lost runs, and a check after every step wins back more, at the cost of time and money. A retry is trying a failed step again. The two columns say how many of 100 runs come out fully right when the check catches a failure and the retry always works, and when the retry works half the time.

*Illustrative numbers, made up for teaching. They are not measurements of any real system.*

| Case | Setup | 100 runs, retry always works | 100 runs, retry works half the time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Ten steps, each right 95 in 100 | 60 (59.9) | 60 |
| B | Nine steps at 95%, one at 80% | 50 (50.4) | 50 |
| C | As B, plus a 90% check on the weak step | 62 (61.8) | 56 (56.1) |
| D | As B, plus that check after every step | 94 (93.7) | 69 (69.2) |

The arithmetic. A check that catches 90% of errors turns a step that is right with chance p into p + (1 - p) x 0.90 with a perfect retry, or p + (1 - p) x 0.90 x 0.5 with a half-working retry.

- Case C, perfect retry: the weak step becomes 0.80 + 0.20 x 0.90 = 0.98, so 0.630 x 0.98 = 0.618.
- Case C, half-working retry: the weak step becomes 0.80 + 0.20 x 0.90 x 0.5 = 0.89, so 0.630 x 0.89 = 0.561.
- Case D, perfect retry: each good step becomes 0.95 + 0.05 x 0.90 = 0.995 and the weak step is 0.98, so 0.995^9 x 0.98 = 0.937.
- Case D, half-working retry: each good step becomes 0.9725 and the weak step is 0.89, so 0.9725^9 x 0.89 = 0.692.

Case C shows that a single check on the worst step gives back about eleven runs in a hundred. Case D shows that checks everywhere help most, but each check costs time and money, and a check that is itself wrong has its own error rate. A check that fails good work (a false positive) adds retries and cost. The numbers say where to look first. They do not say the real answer.

</details>

<details class="go-deeper">
<summary>Go deeper: engineers' extras (monitoring, and restart after a failure)</summary>

**What to monitor.** Keep it to a handful of numbers per run:

| Signal | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Runs started and runs finished | A gap means runs die quietly |
| Check failures per step | Shows the weak step |
| Retries per step | Rising retries mean a step is going bad |
| Time per run and per step | Slow steps warn before a failure |
| Spend per run | Catches a runaway before the bill |
| Time waiting for a human | An unanswered checkpoint is a stuck job |

**Restart and partial failure.** What if the run dies after step 6 of ten? Without planning, you rerun from step 1, which costs time and may repeat side effects (changes outside the run, such as a sent message). A run you can leave alone has these properties:

1. **Each step saves its output** to a named place, with the run identity. Write to a temporary file, then rename it, so a crash never leaves a half-written file that looks complete.
2. **On restart, the run finds the last completed step** and resumes from the next one.
3. **Steps with side effects are [idempotent](../../glossary/#idempotent)**, which means safe to do twice, or they check a record first ("did I already send this?"). Reading and calculating usually are. Sending and paying usually are not.
4. **Steps that cannot be undone sit behind a human checkpoint**, after the checks, as in level 4.
5. **A half-finished run says so.** It leaves a status of "stopped at step 6, waiting".

Two more patterns help. In the outbox pattern, a step writes the message it intends to send into a record, and a separate sender sends from that record and marks it sent. If two runs can start with the same run identity, a lease (a claim with an expiry time) stops both acting at once.

So if Mina's run dies after the calculate step, the saved totals are still there. The restart skips reading and calculating, runs the check on the saved totals, and carries on. Nothing was sent, because sending is the last step, behind a checkpoint.

</details>

<details class="go-deeper">
<summary>Go deeper: how many runs is enough?</summary>

In plain words: a few good runs prove little. The sampling rule and its other numbers are in [using an assistant at work](../professional-guardrails/#checking-and-sampling).

Thirty clean runs in a row means the failure rate is probably below about 1 in 10 (the rule of three: 3 divided by 30; exact upper bound 9.5% at 95% confidence). That is a loose bound.

</details>

<details class="go-deeper">
<summary>Go deeper: what counts as a failure at month-end</summary>

Set the tolerance before the run. A reconciliation clears to 0.00 with every item named and traced to a source. A rounding difference of up to 1.00 is allowed only as a named rounding item that the approver initials. Any other difference stops the close.

A budget line past the [investigation threshold](../../glossary/#investigation-threshold) needs an explanation. In this guide's example that is a line more than 5% and more than 50.00 from budget. The threshold is the size below which a difference does not need an explanation. The size of error that would change a reader's decision about the whole accounts is a different idea, and the accountant judges it. For a long list of items, review every item above a set amount and a random sample of the rest, and write down how you chose the sample. Never sample final totals or reconciling items. The [professional guardrails](../professional-guardrails/) page has the full rule.

</details>

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
Independence is the weak assumption in the chain. Correlated failures, such as one bad input feeding several steps, make the real end-to-end rate differ from the product, and a check that shares the producer's model and prompt often shares its blind spots (see [level 5](../05-check-the-work/)). A check is worth its cost only if its errors are mostly independent of the thing it checks, so a cheap deterministic check (a count, a schema, a sum against the bank statement) beats a second model asking "does this look right?".

Bounded retries need backoff (a pause that grows) and a failure class. Retrying a deterministic wrong answer burns spend. Retrying a timeout is sensible. Cap total attempts per run, not only per step.

Idempotency keys on side-effecting steps make a restart safe where the provider drops repeated keys. Where none exists, a crash between the send and the log can send twice.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: running many steps with a log and limits.**

If no cap is given for a multi-step run, ask for one before you start. Ask once per task, in one message, with every open item and your default for each (at most five items). If nobody can answer, state your assumptions and carry on with read-only work. For any action that changes something, stop and report, and take no action. Use a run identity: a string you generate once at the start and reuse on every log line.

Write one audit log line per step, as the step completes. Put `t=` first, in UTC with seconds. Include the run identity, step name, status and a short reason on failure. The wrapper or harness writes the duration and the cost. The model supplies the step name and the status. Do not log secrets or full personal data.

Enforce the time, spend, step and retry caps given to you. When a cap is reached, stop and report. Do not extend a cap yourself.

Retry only failures that look transient. Do not retry a failed check. Retry at most the number of times you are told.

Before a step that has a side effect outside the run, check whether it already ran for this run identity. If it did, skip it. On restart, resume from the last completed step and report the step you resumed from.

Check: after any run, the log lets someone name the step that produced a given output.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Trusting the average step.** Each step looks fine, so the job looks fine. Fix: multiply, then find the weakest step. Put a check there first. Measure with known cases, as in level 5.

**Logging nothing, or logging everything.** With no log, Friday's odd result is a mystery. With a full transcript of everything, nobody reads it and it holds data it should not. Fix: log one short line per step, with status, reason, duration and cost.

**Setting no limits because "it has never run away".** The first runaway is the one that costs real money or sends the same message forty times. Fix: set time, spend, step and retry caps before you leave the run alone. Put anything irreversible behind a human checkpoint.

## Checkpoint

<p class="audience"><em>Audience: the reader. Agents may skip the quiz.</em></p>

You can now:

- Work out the chance a many-step job is fully right, and say what the number does and does not tell you.
- Put a check on the weakest step and explain what it buys.
- Write an audit log line for a step, and use the log to trace a bad output.
- Set time, spend, step and retry limits.
- Say how a run behaves when it dies after step 6.

**Self-test**

1. A five-step job has every step at 90%. Roughly what share of runs are fully right, and what is the first thing you would do about it?
2. Mina's run stopped after the check failed. The log's read line shows `months=9`, and the duplicate month first appears at the calculate step. Where do you look, and where do you not?
3. A run sends a summary email as its final step, then crashes while writing the log. You restart it. What must be true so Mina does not get two emails?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 0.9^5 = 0.59, about six in ten. Find the weakest step by counting on known cases, then add a check there or improve that step. Do not assume all five are equally weak.
2. The read line shows `months=9`, so look at the calculate brief and its inputs, not the export. Do not re-export the data. The log has narrowed the search.
3. Write the intent record before the send. Send with a key the provider uses to drop repeats where one exists. If none exists, accept that a crash can send twice and keep the send behind a human checkpoint.

</details>
