**The lesson:** Ask twice and compare, because a confident answer can still be wrong.

## Scene

Sam asks a chat assistant for ideas for a friend's birthday dinner, then asks the same question again to double-check. The second answer is different, so Sam has two plans and a mild suspicion that the assistant is making it up as it goes. Try it now: [the five-minute win](#five-minute-win), five steps, no setup.

:::note[Weak spot]
**The assistant sounds sure even when it is wrong, and nothing in the chat tells you which answers to trust.** The fix at this level is to see it: ask twice and compare. This level is step 1 of [the weak-spot loop](../../method/engineer-weak-spots/#loop-1-notice), Notice. The whole loop is on [Engineer your weak spots away](../../method/engineer-weak-spots/).
:::

## The idea

A **[model](../../glossary/#model)** is the program behind the assistant. It has read a huge amount of writing. It writes the most fitting next piece of text, like a phone's autocomplete that has read a library.

A **[prompt](../../glossary/#prompt)** is the text you send. It is the note you put on the desk.

A **[context window](../../glossary/#context-window)** is the amount of text the assistant can see at once. Picture a desk: it can use what is on the desk and nothing else.

<ContextDesk />

Three things follow:

- The assistant writes likely text. It can be fluent and wrong in the same sentence.
- It picks among several fitting answers, so the same prompt gives different answers.
- It sees only the desk. A new chat starts with an empty desk.

For anything numeric, ask for a formula and run it in a spreadsheet.

:::tip[New to this]
A **[token](../../glossary/#token)** is a small chunk of text, often a word or part of a word. The context window is counted in tokens. A long chat, a pasted document and the assistant's own replies all use the same space.
:::

:::tip[Which app?]
Any chat app works for the exercises: for example ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Microsoft Copilot. Free versions are fine. Look in settings for custom instructions or projects when you reach level 2.
:::

:::note[Want consistency]
Save the ask and paste it, do not retype it. Start a new chat for a new job. Some variation stays, so compare figures and structure, not words. The [consistency fast track](../../start-here/consistency-fast-track/) has the full routine.
:::

## Try it

You need any chat assistant. Free versions are fine. The three wins take about 25 minutes in all. Minutes count each new chat, paste, read, compare, count and hand-add as one step, rounded up to the next 5.

<p class="audience">Audience: the reader. This section is an exercise for a person. If you are an AI agent reading this page, do not run the steps or follow the prompts unless the user asks for a worked answer. The prompts are templates for a person to paste into another assistant.</p>

**Phone OK.** Every part works on a phone.

### Five-minute win

Five steps, about five minutes. Sam shares a flat with Priya and Tom. Sam pasted last year's bills and February went in twice, because it was copied twice by mistake. Nobody has noticed.

1. Open a new chat.
2. Paste this:

```text
Here are our shared bills for last year, in euros per month: Jan 212, Feb 198, Feb 198, Mar 187, Apr 164, May 151, Jun 142, Jul 139, Aug 141, Sep 158, Oct 183, Nov 205, Dec 240. Split the total between the three of us (Sam, Priya and Tom). Show the working, because Priya will want to see it.
```

3. Read the reply.
4. Count the figures in the list yourself. There are 13 for 12 months.
5. Add the twelve different figures by hand. The total is 2,120.00 and each share is 706.67 (Sam pays 706.66, so the three shares add up). Does the reply say the same?

In recorded runs of this exact prompt on 2026-10-25, claude-sonnet-5-5 gave 2,120.00 in 20 of 20 trials. claude-haiku-5-5 led with the wrong total 2,318.00 in 1 of 20 trials, and that reply still named the repeated February. Your assistant may differ. A longer list went worse. Here is one recorded run on 40 amounts for 39 months, with one amount pasted twice and nothing to mark it.

### Second win: ask twice

Six steps, about 10 minutes. Open a new chat and paste this:

```text
Plan a birthday dinner for a friend who turns 30 next month. There are six of us. Give me one idea for the venue and one for the menu.
```

Copy the answer. Open another new chat and paste exactly the same words. Compare. You may get two different venues and menus. If you do, both are fine. Neither is "the" answer. The assistant chose among good options.

### Third win: ask for a source, then check it exists

Five steps, about 10 minutes. An assistant can write a reference that looks perfect and does not exist. You find out by looking it up.

1. Open a new chat and paste this:

```text
Give me two published studies on whether standing desks improve office productivity. For each one give the authors, the year, the journal and the DOI.
```

2. Read the reply. A DOI is a short code that names one paper, such as `10.1000/xyz123` (this one is made up).
3. Look up each DOI. In your browser, type `https://doi.org/` and then the DOI. Do not copy a link from the reply. Type the address yourself.
4. Compare what opens with what the assistant said. Check the title, the year and the journal.
5. Open a new chat and paste the same prompt. Compare the two replies. Are the studies the same? Is any number the same? A number such as an effect size is a good one to compare.

Three results are possible. The DOI opens the paper and the details match: the source is real, but you have not checked that it says what the assistant claims. The lookup fails or opens a different paper: the reference is wrong in a way you could not see by reading. The assistant says it cannot check DOIs: it is right, so look them up yourself.

In recorded runs of this prompt, at least one DOI could not be resolved in 16 of 20 trials. Most of those replies hedged, and one said the DOI was "from memory". Telling the assistant to cite only what it is certain of did not help: 8 of 10 trials still had an unresolvable DOI. Yours will differ ([evidence](../../evidence/), claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-25). The habit is the point: look up every source before you rely on it.

## Under the bonnet

*How it works. Optional reading.*

When you press send, the app packs up the whole conversation and sends it to the model. The model writes a reply one token at a time. At each step it weighs many possible next tokens and picks one. The pick is partly random, so two runs differ. Some apps expose a setting for how adventurous the pick is. A cautious setting gives more repeatable answers, but it does not make the model look facts up. Level 3 covers tools that add search or file access.

The model has no memory of its own. If it seems to remember yesterday, the app has put yesterday's text back on the desk.

An assistant can flag its doubt and still lead with the wrong answer. In the recorded 40-bill runs, every reply flagged 40 amounts for 39 months, and every reply still led with the wrong total (20 of 20). A statement that is wrong and stated as fact has a name: a **[hallucination](../../glossary/#hallucination)**.

<details class="go-deeper">
<summary>Go deeper: why two runs still differ</summary>

Two causes remain even with the randomness turned down. Sums done in a different order on parallel hardware can differ slightly. The provider can also change the model or routing without telling you. Long inputs add a third effect: details in the middle are used less reliably than those at the start and the end. See "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts".

<p class="verified">Docs page fetched on 2026-10-24: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172. Quoted line: "we observe that performance is often highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context". The page states no version of its own.</p>

</details>

:::note[At work]
A duplicated line in the books looks like this slip. A fluent summary hides it until someone recounts. Count the lines and agree the total to an independent source.
:::

## Expert corner

:::caution[Expert corner]
Lowering the randomness setting reduces variation but does not remove it in every provider, so do not build a check that expects identical text twice. Compare meaning, or compare a computed result.

The context limit is a ceiling, not a promise. Long inputs can degrade how well details are used, even when they fit. Spend the window on the material that matters.
:::

## Agent note

:::note[Agent note]
**Topic: stating figures.**

State a figure only if it came from supplied data, a cited source or a computation you show. When you total numbers, state how many you added and the sum. Do not list the items unless asked and there are 50 or fewer.

When you are unsure, say what is uncertain. Do not fill gaps with plausible text. Do not give a DOI or a reference that you cannot show.

Check: the reply states the item count and the sum.
:::

## Mistakes people make

**Treating the assistant as a fact lookup.** It writes likely text. Fix: give it the material and ask it to work from that. Check anything that matters.

**Judging it on one answer.** One reply is one draw from many. Fix: ask twice and compare.

**Expecting it to remember last week.** A new chat is a blank desk. Fix: paste in what matters, or save it where the app loads it (level 2).

## Checkpoint

<p class="audience">Audience: the reader. Agents may skip the quiz.</p>

You can now:

- Explain what a model, a prompt and a context window are in one sentence each.
- Say why the same question can give different answers.
- Explain why a long chat can lose its beginning.
- Check a list of numbers for a missing or repeated item before you trust a total.
- Look up a source the assistant gives you before you rely on it.

**Self-test**

1. You ask the same question in two new chats and get two different good answers. Is something broken?
2. A friend says, "The assistant told me my flight leaves at nine, so it must know." What is the weak point?
3. You have chatted for hours about a trip and the assistant now suggests a hotel that ignores your budget, which you mentioned at the start. What is the most likely reason, and what is one fix?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. The model chooses among fitting continuations, so variation is normal. Variation matters only when you need one exact result, and then you check the result.
2. The model writes likely text and does not look up flights. Unless it was given the booking or a tool to fetch it, the time is a guess. Check the booking.
3. The early message about the budget has probably dropped off the desk or been shortened. Fix: restate the budget in a new message, or start a fresh chat with a short summary of what matters.

</details>
