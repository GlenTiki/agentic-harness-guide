**Recommendation:** when a task depends on an expert's judgement, interview the expert on a few rare, high-stakes cases, write what you learn as a decision table, and test the table directly. Do not expect more documents to supply this.

## Why it matters

Principle, not measured: an expert's skill sits in what they notice and in how they handle the case the written process does not fit. A procedure document records the common path. An assistant that reads ten more procedure documents learns the common path better and still lacks the exception.

The usual symptom is an answer that reads well and is wrong in the one case that mattered. Judging the answer by its prose will not catch it. You need to know which rule the assistant applied. That is why this page ends in a test on the rule, not on the wording.

## What to enable

**1. Audit broadly and shallowly first.** Before you interview anyone, list the decisions in the work and ask for one line each: what is decided, how often, what goes wrong. The aim is to choose which cases to probe. Pick the rare ones where a mistake costs the most, because those are the ones the written process handles worst.

**2. Interview on one real case.** Pick a case that happened. Ask four questions, in this order, and let the expert talk.

1. What did you notice?
2. What did you consider?
3. What would a novice have done?
4. What would have changed your mind?

The third question brings out the cue the expert uses without naming it. The fourth gives you the edge of the rule, which is where a decision table needs its rows.

**3. Watch the work as well as asking.** People describe their process, and their process as they describe it is neater than what they do. Sit with the expert for a few real cases and write down what they open, what they skip and what makes them stop. A rare case may not turn up while you watch. That is why the interview on a past case comes first, and the observation checks the common cases against what was said.

**4. Spot a rule the owner cannot state.** Two signs:

- The written process says one thing and the expert does another. In the interview above, the process says "check the total" and she checks the bank details.
- The expert says "it just looks wrong" or "I would not do that". Ask what you would see if it were right, and what you would see if it were wrong. Keep asking for a visible cue until you can point at it on the page.

Record each gap as a note: the written step, what was done, the cue. Each note is a candidate row.

**5. Record it as a decision table.** A table has three parts: the cues you can read from the case, the options on offer, and the expert's choice. Keep each cue to a value you can read off the input, such as yes or no, not a feeling. Order the rows and say that the first match wins, so the order is part of the rule.

**6. Test the table, not the prose.** For each row, write at least one case that must fire it. Add a case for each cue, with that cue missing, because a payment row must need every prerequisite cue. A missing cue must lead to "ask, do not pay", never to a payment. Give the table a last row that fires when nothing else does, and make that row the safe one. Then check which row fired, separately from whether the answer text sounds right. The script below does the check for a table in a file.

**7. Treat a surprise as a signal.** Log each question the system could not answer and each time the expert overrode it. Each entry is a missing row or a missing cue, not a missing document. Review the log on a fixed day, and turn the repeats into rows.

## How much have you captured

Two rough ideas to measure on your own data. They are not results, and nothing here says what a good number is.

- **Rows.** The count of rows in the table, and how many were added in each interview. When new interviews stop adding rows, you have probably covered the cases that come up. When they keep adding rows, you have not.
- **Time.** Give one expert and one novice, with the table, the same ten cases. Compare the time and the number of wrong choices. If the table helps the novice little, the missing part is in the cues, not the choices.

## Failure modes it reduces

- A fluent answer that applies the common rule to a rare case.
- A document that says "check the total" while the owner checks something else.
- A table that nobody can test, so the first sign of a wrong row is a wrong payment.
- A rule that only exists in one person's head and leaves with them.

## First step for the maintainer

Choose one decision that an expert in your work makes by feel. Ask for one real, rare case where it went wrong or nearly did. Run the four questions, write three to five rows, add one case per missing cue with a safe fallback row, and run the check.
